If someone makes a fake nude or sexual video of you, for example by using software or AI to put your face or body into it, and then shares or publishes it without your consent, you can sue them in New York. You can sue anyone who shares it when they knew, or should have known, that you didn't agree. A disclaimer saying the video is fake doesn't protect them.

A court may order the material stopped and award you damages, including punitive damages, plus court costs and lawyer's fees. You don't need a police report or a criminal conviction first. You have three years after the video is shared to sue, or one year after you find it, whichever is later. Consent counts only if you signed a plain-language agreement describing the material, and you can usually take it back in writing within three business days. Reporting unlawful activity, law-enforcement duties, legal proceedings, material of legitimate public concern, and constitutionally protected news, commentary or criticism are exempt.

Sen. Diane Savino sponsored the bill and Assemblymember Helene Weinstein carried its Assembly companion. The Senate passed it 60–0 on July 22, 2020, and the Assembly passed it 140–1 the next day, with yes votes from both parties. The same law also protects the names and likenesses of deceased performers from unauthorized commercial use. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on November 30, 2020, and it took effect on May 29, 2021.
