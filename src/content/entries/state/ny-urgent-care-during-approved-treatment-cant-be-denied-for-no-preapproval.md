Sometimes, partway through a treatment your health plan already approved, your provider finds you need something more right away: medicine for nausea or an allergic reaction during chemotherapy, say, or care for a related condition. Since March 2020, New York law says a state-regulated plan can't refuse to pay for that extra care just because no one paused to get it approved separately. The provider has to judge that it was immediately necessary and that stopping treatment to ask for approval wasn't medically advisable.

You don't have to apply for this. It covers insurers, nonprofit plans like Blue Cross plans, HMOs, and municipal cooperative health plans regulated by the state. If the plan denies the extra care anyway and the denial is appealed, the denial can stand only for a few reasons: the service isn't a covered benefit, it wasn't medically necessary, it was experimental, or another narrow exception in the law applies, such as fraud or false information on the original request.

A 2007 state law already protected closely related procedures done under sedation. State Sen. Neil Breslin and Assemblymember Pamela Hunter carried this bill to widen it, after plans' contractors kept denying related care that fell outside that narrow rule. Versions had been introduced since 2015. It passed unanimously in June 2019, 62–0 in the Senate and 149–0 in the Assembly. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on December 12, 2019, and it took effect 90 days later.
