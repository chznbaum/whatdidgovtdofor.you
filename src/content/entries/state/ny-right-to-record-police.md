Across New York State, you have a legal right to record police and other officers engaged in law enforcement activity on streets, sidewalks, in parks and in other open public spaces, using video, photos, audio or written notes. If you are not under arrest or in custody, you also have the right to keep control of the recording and the device you used. If you are arrested or in custody, that status alone does not erase your right to have existing recordings and equipment maintained and returned. The law does not protect physically interfering with officers or conduct that is otherwise a crime involving obstruction. In June 2026, New York's highest court ruled that the law does not apply inside police stationhouses, including their publicly accessible lobbies.

You do not have to sign up for anything. If an officer unlawfully prevents or threatens you from recording, orders you to stop when you are authorized to record, searches, tickets or arrests you because you recorded, or unlawfully takes, destroys or copies your recording, you can sue. A court may award damages, including punitive damages, grant an injunction and let a prevailing plaintiff recover reasonable attorney and expert fees. You have three years to bring a case.

Assemblymember Nick Perry introduced an Assembly version of the Right to Monitor Act in 2014. Sen. Kevin Parker introduced the first Senate version in 2016, and each sponsored the final version in their chamber in 2020. Lawmakers passed it shortly after George Floyd's murder: 59–3 in the Senate and 128–16 in the Assembly. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on June 14, 2020, and it took effect July 14.
