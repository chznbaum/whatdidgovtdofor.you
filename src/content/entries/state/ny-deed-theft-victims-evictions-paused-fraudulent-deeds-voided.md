Deed theft happens when a scammer forges your signature, or tricks you into signing your home away, and then records the deed as their own. Victims used to face eviction or foreclosure while their case dragged on. This law lets New York's attorney general, district attorneys and other investigators ask a court to pause any eviction, foreclosure or ownership case involving the home. The court must grant the pause while an investigation is under way or charges have been filed.

The law also gives prosecutors more tools. They can file a public warning on the property's records so buyers and lenders know there's a title problem. After a conviction, they can ask a court to void the fraudulent deed or loan. In civil cases, a deed theft conviction makes the transfer presumed fraudulent, and buyers who should have known about the fraud lose protection. There is no program application. Report suspected theft to the attorney general, your district attorney or the sheriff. If a scammer is trying to evict you, you can ask the housing court for a 90-day pause to file a case challenging ownership.

Attorney General Letitia James drafted the bill. Sen. Brian Kavanagh and Assemblymember Helene Weinstein carried it. Sen. Zellnor Myrie co-sponsored the Senate bill and worked on the 2023 deed theft protections. The Senate passed it 61–0 in June 2023, and the Assembly passed it that month. Gov. Kathy Hochul signed it on Nov. 14, 2023.
