Probation can drag on for years. It means check-ins, fees, and permission slips to leave the state, and those can get in the way of holding a job. Since July 1, 2026, Virginia law treats ending supervision early as a reward for doing well. If you're placed on supervised probation for more than a year, and the Department of Corrections finds that you have kept the court's conditions, followed your case plan, and pose minimal risk to the community, it must ask the court to end supervision after 12 months—unless a court has ordered otherwise or another law bars the request.

For this route, you don't file the request yourself. The Department of Corrections makes it, and a judge decides. The law doesn't cover people whose sentence requires at least three years of supervised probation, or people supervised by local community-based probation agencies. The department must make reasonable efforts to notify victims when supervision ends this way. The law also leaves intact courts' other authority to shorten or end probation, and it allows a judge to act without a hearing on the department's request.

Republican Sen. Christie New Craig of Chesapeake and Republican Del. Wren Williams of Patrick County carried the identical bills. REFORM Alliance credits Democrats Katrina Callsen and Barbara Favola with sustained leadership and says its late Virginia organizer Sincere B. Allah helped drive the work. Prosecutors, probation officers, victims' advocates, defense attorneys, and reform groups shaped this version after earlier attempts fell short. With support from both parties, SB 136 passed the House 82–15, and the Senate agreed to the House version 32–8. Gov. Abigail Spanberger signed it on April 6, 2026.
