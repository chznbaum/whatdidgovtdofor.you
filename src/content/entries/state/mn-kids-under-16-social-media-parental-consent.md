Starting July 1, 2027, covered social media platforms generally need a parent's consent before a Minnesota child 15 or younger can open or keep an account. The law covers platforms with at least 10,000 account holders or at least $1 billion in worldwide revenue in one or more of the preceding three years. It exempts platforms from taking action on accounts held continuously for at least seven years. A child's covered account starts at the most private settings, and the platform can't loosen them. The account can't display infinite scroll, autoplay or other features the law defines as addictive, or targeted paid commercial ads. Platforms generally can't sell or share information collected on a child's account.

When a platform asks for consent, it must offer parents tools to track time spent and set daily, weekly and time-of-day limits. Platforms must estimate the ages of newer users after they have spent enough time on the service and treat users they cannot confidently place at 16 or older as children. A platform must close a child's account within seven days of the child's request or within 14 days of a parent's request. A child or parent who prevails over a reckless or knowing violation receives actual damages or $10,000, whichever is greater. Platforms that used reasonable compliance efforts are not liable. The attorney general can also enforce the law, and contracts cannot waive its protections.

Rep. Peggy Scott, a Republican, carried the bill in the House, and Sen. Erin Maye Quade, a Democrat, carried it in the Senate. Both parties backed it: the Senate passed it 66–0 and the House gave final approval 131–2. Gov. Tim Walz signed it on May 26, 2026. NetChoice predicted that Minnesota would be sued and that a federal court would block the law before it takes effect.
