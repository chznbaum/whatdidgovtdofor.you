New York requires children to get vaccines such as measles, polio, mumps and chickenpox before they attend school or child care. Until 2019, parents could skip them by claiming a religious belief. This law ended that exemption. Now an unvaccinated child can attend only with a medical exemption, given when a doctor says a vaccine would be unsafe for that child. The rule covers public, private and religious schools, day care, nursery school and pre-K.

That protects the children who can't be vaccinated, such as kids in cancer treatment, along with infants and others who catch disease from unvaccinated classmates. In the 2017–18 school year, 26,217 students had religious exemptions. Families don't need to do anything if their children are already up to date.

Assembly Member Jeffrey Dinowitz and Sen. Brad Hoylman (now Hoylman-Sigal) carried the bill. Advocates had pushed for repeal since 2015. Assembly Speaker Carl Heastie helped steward it through the Assembly, and Senate Majority Leader Andrea Stewart-Cousins was credited for her leadership and support. It passed during the state's worst measles outbreak since 1992, with more than 924 confirmed cases. Both chambers passed it on June 13, 2019, the Assembly 84–61 and the Senate 36–26. In the Senate, five Democrats voted no and one Republican voted yes. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it that same day.

Federal courts have upheld the law. A challenge from Amish families is now asking the U.S. Supreme Court to hear it.
