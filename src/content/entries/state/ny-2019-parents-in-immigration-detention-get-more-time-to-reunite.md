When a child has been in foster care for 15 of the last 22 months, New York generally requires the local social services agency to ask a family court to end the parents' rights for good. The law already allowed exceptions for parents who were in prison or in residential drug treatment. The Reuniting Families Act adds parents who are in immigration detention or facing deportation proceedings, including cases where detention earlier on is a big reason the child is still in care.

The exception has conditions. The parent has to keep a meaningful role in the child's life, and the agency must not have written down a reason that filing to end the parent's rights is still right. Parents don't have to apply for anything. The agency applies the rule when it decides whether to file. The bill's sponsors said parents caught up in immigration cases often can't take their kids back through no fault of their own, and that ending their rights during those cases could separate families permanently.

State Sen. Kevin Parker of Brooklyn first introduced the bill in 2013–14 and brought it back every session until it passed. Assembly Member Ron Kim of Queens carried it in the Assembly. In 2019 the Senate passed it 56–5 and the Assembly 145–1. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on July 27, 2019, and it took effect 90 days later.
