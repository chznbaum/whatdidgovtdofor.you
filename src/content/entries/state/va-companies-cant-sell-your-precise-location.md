Your phone keeps track of where you are, and data brokers have long bought and resold that information. Since July 1, 2026, Virginia law flatly bars companies covered by its consumer privacy law from selling or offering to sell your precise location. That means data that places you within 1,750 feet, such as GPS coordinates. Before, a company could sell it once you gave affirmative consent. Now your consent doesn't make a sale legal.

You don't have to do anything to get this protection. It applies to companies covered by Virginia's privacy law—generally those that do business in Virginia or target Virginians and handle personal data for at least 100,000 Virginia consumers a year, or 25,000 if over half their revenue comes from selling data. The Attorney General enforces it. A company gets 30 days to fix a violation after being notified, and after that it can face civil penalties of up to $7,500 per violation. Companies can still share location data with a contractor working for them or to provide a service you asked for.

Sen. Russet Perry introduced the same protection in 2025; it passed the Senate unanimously but did not advance in the House. She carried it again in 2026, with Sen. David Suetterlein as co-patron, while Consumer Reports led a coalition of privacy and consumer groups supporting it. It passed 96–0 in the House of Delegates, and the Senate agreed to the House amendment 40–0. Gov. Abigail Spanberger signed it on April 13, 2026. Virginia became the third state to ban sales of precise location data, after Maryland and Oregon.
