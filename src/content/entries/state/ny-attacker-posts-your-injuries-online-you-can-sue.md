New York makes it a class A misdemeanor for someone who committed, participated in, or conspired to commit a crime to create and post an identifiable image of the victim on social media, without consent, intending to degrade the victim or harm the victim or their family. The image must show physical injury from a sex offense, serious physical injury from another offense, or death, and have little or no cultural or social value. An agent who, with the same harmful intent, knowingly posts the image for its creator commits the offense too.

The victim can sue the person who violated the law; a court may award damages, an injunction, costs and attorneys' fees. The victim or, after a death, the estate can ask a court to order a social media platform to remove the image. No criminal case is required first. The deadline is three years after posting or one year after discovery, whichever is later. The civil claim excludes specified official uses and legitimate news, commentary, art and public discourse.

The law joined campaigns after the 2009 murder of Caroline Wimmer and the 2019 murder of Bianca Devins, 17, whose body images were posted online. Their mothers, Marti Wimmer and Kim Devins, pushed for change. Sen. Diane Savino worked on Caroline's Law for 11 years with Sen. Andrew Lanza and Assemblymember Michael Cusick; Assemblymember Marianne Buttenschon and Sen. Joe Griffo carried Bianca's Law. Gov. Kathy Hochul signed the combined bill on Dec. 28, 2022. A 2023 amendment sponsored by Buttenschon and Sen. Monica Martinez narrowed it to images created by a participant in the crime and set its final form.
