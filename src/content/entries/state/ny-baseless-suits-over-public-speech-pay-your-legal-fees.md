Some lawsuits are filed to scare people into silence with the cost of defending themselves. New York's 1992 anti-SLAPP law covered little beyond public permits and zoning, and courts rarely made plaintiffs pay defendants' legal bills.

The 2020 law widened protection in New York state court to cover anything you say in a public place or forum about an issue of public interest, along with other lawful acts of speech or petitioning. "Public interest" means anything that isn't a purely private matter. If a court finds the suit lacks a substantial basis in fact and law and cannot be supported by a substantial argument to change existing law, it must order the other side to pay your costs and lawyer fees. Once you file an anti-SLAPP motion to dismiss, discovery and other motions pause until the court rules, with narrow exceptions. You don't apply. You or your lawyer raise it in court.

Assembly Member Helene Weinstein and Sen. Brad Hoylman sponsored the bill. Earlier versions passed the Assembly in 2013, 2015 and 2016 but died in the Senate. In July 2020, it passed the Assembly 116–26 and the Senate 57–3. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on November 10. In 2023, New York's top court held that the fee-and-damages remedy can cover a suit filed before the law if the plaintiff continued it after November 10, 2020, but fees and damages cannot reach back before that date.
