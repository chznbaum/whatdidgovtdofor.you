If a person or business that owns or licenses your covered private information discovers a breach involving unauthorized access or acquisition, New York's main rule now requires it to notify you as quickly as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery. Covered information includes Social Security and driver's license numbers, and bank or card account numbers that can be used to access an account. Law enforcement may delay notice when it would impede a criminal investigation. Notices made under certain federal or state data-security laws, including HIPAA, can satisfy New York's individual-notice rule instead. A service provider that only maintains data for someone else must immediately tell the owner or licensee, with 30 days as the outer limit.

Before this change, the state's general rule required notice “in the most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay,” but set no fixed outer deadline. You do not apply for this protection. Direct notice normally comes in writing, by telephone, or electronically with your consent, although the law permits substitute notice in specified large, costly, or hard-to-contact cases. The law also brought the state Department of Financial Services into breach reporting. A follow-up law in February 2025 clarified that only businesses the department regulates must notify it and that notifying regulators cannot delay notice to residents.

State Sen. Leroy Comrie and Assemblymember Nader Sayegh carried the bill. It passed the Senate 60–0 in June 2024 after an earlier version passed 60–1 in 2023. The Assembly passed it the same month. Gov. Kathy Hochul signed it on December 21, 2024, and it took effect that day.
