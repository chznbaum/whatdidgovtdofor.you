If you live in New York, you have the right to call police or emergency services without being punished for it where you live. A landlord can't evict you, end your tenancy, or refuse to renew your lease because you asked for help, because someone called for you, or because you were the victim of a crime such as domestic violence at home. Local "nuisance" laws can't be used against you for it either, and you can't be made to sign away this right: a lease clause that tries is void.

Before the law, many New York cities, towns and villages had nuisance ordinances that counted 911 calls against a property, whatever the reason for them. Owners facing fines or losing a permit sometimes evicted the tenant who called. The ACLU and NYCLU found that domestic violence was the biggest reason for enforcement in Fulton and Binghamton.

You don't need to apply. If you're taken to court, the law is a defense, and you can sue for damages. If you win, a court may also award costs and attorney's fees. A landlord can still evict an abuser while letting you remain through the rest of the lease, and can still evict for reasons that have nothing to do with calling for help.

Assemblymember Charles Lavine and Sen. Brad Hoylman sponsored the bill. It passed the Assembly 146–0 and the Senate 58–1, and Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on Sept. 13, 2019.
