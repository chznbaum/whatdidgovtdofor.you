Corn tortillas, tamales and pupusas are made from corn masa flour. Under this law, that flour must have folic acid added to it: 0.7 milligrams per pound. The package label must list the folic acid. Getting enough folic acid early in pregnancy, often before someone knows they're pregnant, lowers the chance of spina bifida and other serious birth defects of the brain and spine. Bread, pasta and other enriched grains have had added folic acid under a federal rule since 1998. Neural tube defects fell 35% after that rule, but it left out corn masa flour.

You don't have to do anything to benefit. Companies that make, distribute or sell the flour have to comply, and the deadline is one year after the law took effect. Wet masa makers may fortify too, but they don't have to. Home cottage-food sellers and snack foods are exempt. Grocery stores can still sell unfortified flour as long as they also stock a fortified one. The state health commissioner will write the rules and can grant hardship exemptions.

Senate Majority Leader Teresa Ruiz and Assembly members Yvonne Lopez and Eliana Pintor Marin sponsored the bill. They argued that federal approval of voluntary fortification in 2016 had reached only about 10% of corn masa products, which leaves Latino families at higher risk. Both chambers passed it on June 30, 2026: the Assembly 75–2 with most Republicans in favor, and the Senate 29–11. Gov. Mikie Sherrill signed it on Sept. 28, 2026.
