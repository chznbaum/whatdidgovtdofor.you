When a child with diabetes has dangerously low blood sugar at school and no licensed health professional is available, a trained teacher or other staff member can give the child glucagon, the emergency medicine that raises blood sugar. New York law used to say staff could only *inject* it. This law changed the word to *administer*, so trained staff can also give glucagon that goes in the nose, which needs no needle and no mixing.

Public school districts, BOCES and nonpublic schools can choose to train staff. The law covers emergencies during the school day, on school grounds and at school events. Your child needs a prescription and a written OK from the doctor, and you have to give written consent. A doctor or other licensed health professional trains the staff, following state rules.

The FDA first approved nasal glucagon in July 2019 for people with diabetes ages 4 and up, then expanded approval in March 2025 to ages 1 and up. At the time of the first approval, it said the injectable kits then available had to be mixed in several steps, which is hard to do quickly when someone is unconscious or having a seizure. Sen. Shelley Mayer first introduced the fix in 2020 and carried it again in 2021, with Assemblymember Michael Benedetto sponsoring it in the Assembly. It passed the Senate 62–0 and the Assembly 148–1. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on August 2, 2021, and it took effect right away.
