If you're convicted of a felony in New York, you lose the right to vote only while you're in prison for it. The day you're released, it comes back automatically, whether or not you're on parole. You don't have to apply to get the right back or wait for a pardon, but you must register again before you vote. This applies to New York and federal felony convictions and to out-of-state offenses that would be felonies under New York law.

The law also makes sure people hear about it. Before accepting a guilty plea to certain felony charges, New York courts must explain that the person will lose the vote while in prison and regain it upon release. Before someone with a felony conviction leaves a New York state or local correctional facility, staff must give that notice out loud and in writing. They must also provide a voter registration form and offer help filling it out. The state Board of Elections has to educate judges, lawyers, and parole and election officials about these rules.

Before this law, people on parole couldn't vote. Starting in 2018, Gov. Andrew Cuomo used individual conditional pardons to restore voting rights for most of them, but a later governor could have stopped. Assembly Member Daniel O'Donnell said he first wrote the bill in 2005. He and Sen. Leroy Comrie carried the 2021 versions, and the bill became law after a 16-year fight. It passed the Senate 43–20 in February 2021 and the Assembly 97–52 that April. Cuomo signed it on May 4, 2021. More than 35,000 people were on parole in New York when it passed.
