1,4-dioxane is a chemical by-product that can end up in soaps, detergents and shampoos when they're made. The EPA calls it a likely cause of cancer, and ordinary water treatment can't remove it. It has turned up in Long Island drinking water wells at the highest levels in the state. New York's law limits how much of it products sold in the state may contain. Household cleaners and personal care products such as shampoo and body wash were capped at 2 parts per million at the end of 2022 and at 1 part per million a year later. Cosmetics are capped at 10 parts per million.

You don't have to do anything: the limits apply to what stores can sell. Manufacturers that couldn't meet the limits in time could get up to two one-year waivers, and the last of those ran out on Dec. 30, 2025. Repeat violations can cost up to $2,500 a day. In 2025, state regulators reviewed the limits as the law requires and found them protective enough.

Sen. Todd Kaminsky and Assemblyman Steve Englebright, both Long Island Democrats, carried the bill. Citizens Campaign for the Environment pushed for it after its testing found the chemical in 65 of 80 common household products. Over industry objections, the Senate passed it 62–0. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it in December 2019, and in April 2020 he signed Kaminsky and Englebright's follow-up bill, which made agreed technical corrections to the law's section numbers.
