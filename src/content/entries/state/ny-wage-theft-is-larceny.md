If you do the work and your employer intentionally withholds the minimum wage, the overtime you're owed, or a higher wage you were promised, New York can prosecute that as larceny. Prosecutors still must prove an intent to deprive you of your pay; a payroll mistake alone is not larceny.

The law also changes how the theft is added up. Prosecutors can combine underpayments from one employer to one worker into a single count, even if the work was done in different counties. They can do the same for a workforce shorted under one plan. That matters because larceny gets more serious as the total grows. More than $1,000 can be felony grand larceny, and higher totals can bring longer prison sentences. Small underpayments can add up to a serious case. Workers do not apply for a criminal charge; prosecutors decide whether the evidence supports one.

Assemblymember Catalina Cruz first introduced the bill in 2019 and carried it for three legislative sessions; Sen. Neil Breslin carried the Senate version. Former Manhattan prosecutor Diana Florence helped write it, and the New York City District Council of Carpenters helped build the coalition that pushed it through. The Senate passed it 61–0 with support from both parties, and the Assembly passed it 140–4. Gov. Kathy Hochul signed it on Sept. 6, 2023, and it took effect that day. Cruz cited an estimate that $3.2 billion is stolen from more than 2 million New Yorkers' paychecks each year.
