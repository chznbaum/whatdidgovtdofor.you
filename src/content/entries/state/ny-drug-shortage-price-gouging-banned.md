When a medicine runs short, sellers can be tempted to raise prices sharply on patients and hospitals that can't wait. New York's price-gouging law now treats a drug shortage like a storm or other emergency. If the U.S. Food and Drug Administration publicly reports that a drug or medical product is in shortage, no one in the supply chain may charge an unconscionably excessive price for it. That covers manufacturers, wholesalers, distributors and pharmacies.

You don't have to do anything to be protected. The state attorney general enforces the law. A price far above what the seller charged just before the shortage, or far above what others nearby charge, is evidence of gouging, though a seller can show its own costs went up. A court can order refunds to the people who were overcharged and a penalty of up to $25,000 per violation, or three times what the seller took in, whichever is more.

State Sen. Julia Salazar and Assemblymember Karines Reyes, a registered nurse, carried the bill. An earlier version passed both houses in 2022 but was vetoed. The 2023 bill passed the Senate 62–0 with cosponsors from both parties, and the Assembly passed it the same day in May. Gov. Kathy Hochul signed it on December 13, 2023, and it took effect immediately.
