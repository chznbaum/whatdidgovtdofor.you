When relatives inherit a house or farm together, often because there was no will, each owns a share. Under the old rules, one could ask a court to sell the whole property even if the others wanted to keep it. Speculators exploited that by buying a small share and then seeking a forced sale, sometimes for far less than the property was worth.

This law created special rules for qualifying “heirs property”: a home or farm held as tenants in common, with no binding partition agreement, a share acquired from a relative, and enough family ownership or an inheriting resident. The court sets fair market value, usually through an appraisal. Co-owners who did not seek a sale get 45 days after notice of that value to choose to buy the sale-seeking shares. If they do not, the court must divide the property unless that would badly hurt the owners as a group. If sale is ordered, it usually begins with an open-market listing at the court-set value. A judge can instead use sealed bids or an auction when that better serves the group, and can approve the highest offer if the listing does not reach that value. The protections apply automatically in a partition case.

Sen. Brad Hoylman and Assembly Member Jeffrey Dinowitz sponsored the bill. Their memo said lower- and middle-income families were especially vulnerable because they often lacked estate plans. Sen. Velmanette Montgomery co-sponsored it and highlighted Black homeowners facing displacement. The Senate passed it 62–0 and the Assembly 103–38. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on Dec. 6, 2019.
