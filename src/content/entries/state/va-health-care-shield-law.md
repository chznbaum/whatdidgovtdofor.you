Some states treat health care that's legal in Virginia as a crime. This law keeps Virginia law enforcement from helping investigate it. Officers can't investigate, arrest or detain anyone, seek a warrant, pass along information, or otherwise support an investigation involving protected care. To qualify, the care must be lawful in Virginia and involve a provider licensed in Virginia and physically present in the state. The protection also covers people who help someone provide or receive that care.

If another state charges someone, the governor generally can't extradite them over protected care. That protection does not apply if the person was in the charging state when the alleged offense occurred and then fled, or if the conduct would also be a crime in Virginia. Foreign-subpoena requests must state under oath whether they seek documents, information or testimony about protected care, and Virginia courts cannot summon witnesses for related out-of-state criminal cases.

The law includes abortion and gender-affirming care when they are legal in Virginia. You don't have to apply. If someone harms you through an out-of-state legal action meant to punish protected care, or violates the law's attestation rules, you can sue. Compensatory damages awarded under that section are at least $5,000. The Attorney General can seek up to $10,000 per violation.

Sen. Russet Perry was chief patron, with Sen. Barbara Favola as co-patron. The bill passed the Senate 20–19 and the House 60–35. Every vote cast by a Democrat was yes, and every vote cast by a Republican was no. Gov. Abigail Spanberger signed it on April 13, 2026. Two years earlier, Gov. Glenn Youngkin vetoed shield bills that would have blocked extradition of reproductive health providers. The law took effect July 1, 2026.
