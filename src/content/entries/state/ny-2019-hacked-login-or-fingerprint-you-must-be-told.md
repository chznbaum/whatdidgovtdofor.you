If a company holds your personal information and hackers get into it, New York law says you have to be told. The SHIELD Act widened the kinds of information that count. Before, they were mostly Social Security, driver's license and account numbers. Now they also include your fingerprints or other biometric data, and a username or email address combined with its password. A breach no longer requires stolen data: unauthorized access counts too. The notice rules cover any business or person holding a New York resident's data, even one based in another state.

The law also requires those businesses to protect the data. They need real safeguards, such as staff in charge of security, regular testing of their controls, and deleting data they no longer need. Small businesses can use safeguards that fit their size. You don't have to do anything to be covered. You can't sue under the law yourself; the attorney general enforces it and can seek up to $5,000 per violation for weak security and, for knowingly or recklessly failing to send breach notices, up to $250,000.

Attorney General Letitia James's office wrote the bill. Sen. Kevin Thomas, who chaired the Senate Consumer Protection Committee, and Assembly Member Michael DenDekker carried it in the legislature. The final version passed the Senate 41–21 and passed the Assembly the same day, June 17, 2019. Gov. Andrew Cuomo signed it on July 25, 2019, shortly after the settlement over the Equifax breach, which exposed data on nearly 150 million people. The notice changes took effect that October and the security rules in March 2020.
