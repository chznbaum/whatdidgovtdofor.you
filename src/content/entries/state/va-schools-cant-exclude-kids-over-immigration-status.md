Every child in Virginia has the right to a free public education through high school, no matter what their immigration or citizenship status is, or what a school thinks it is. The same goes for their parents' status. The law puts into state law the 1982 Supreme Court ruling in Plyler v. Doe. Schools can't turn a child away from enrolling or keep them out of programs and activities on that basis. They can't collect immigration papers unless another law requires it, list a student's birthplace or nationality as directory information, or threaten to share a family's status with police or immigration agents. Schools can still follow federal information-sharing laws.

You don't have to apply for this protection. If a school breaks the law, an affected person can sue for a court order and, if the violation was willful, actual damages. A plaintiff who wins gets reasonable lawyer's fees paid. Normally, the person must first give the school division written notice and wait 15 days while it investigates and, if warranted, fixes the problem. A court can grant temporary or preliminary relief without that notice when a student is currently being unlawfully excluded or immediate, irreparable harm is likely. Lawsuits must be filed within two years. School boards must adopt policies by Dec. 31, 2026, and principals and administrators must be trained by the 2027-28 school year.

Del. Sam Rasoul, who chairs the House Education Committee, carried the bill, and it took in a similar bill from Del. Alfonso Lopez. Sen. Stella Pekarsky carried an identical Senate version. They acted after immigration arrests rose and the federal government ended a policy that had discouraged arrests near schools. The final version passed the House 62-34 and the Senate 21-18. Gov. Abigail Spanberger signed it on April 13, 2026.
