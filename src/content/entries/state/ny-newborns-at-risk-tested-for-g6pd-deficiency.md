G6PD deficiency is an inherited condition that makes red blood cells easier to damage. In newborns, that can cause severe jaundice; if bilirubin rises too high, it can damage the brain. Most people who carry a G6PD variant do not know it. Finding the condition lets clinicians check bilirubin before it becomes dangerous and helps families avoid foods and medicines that can trigger red-cell breakdown.

New York law requires a diagnostic G6PD test in three situations: the baby has hemolytic anemia or hemolytic jaundice, or early-onset jaundice that is increasing, above the 40th percentile for the baby's age in hours, and persists after the day of birth during the following week; the baby is admitted to a hospital for jaundice after birth; or a biological parent reports a family, racial, or ethnic risk, including significant African, Asian, Mediterranean, or Middle Eastern ancestry. Parents do not have to apply. Telling the hospital about family history or ancestry is enough to qualify the baby.

State Sen. Gustavo Rivera and Assembly Member Richard Gottfried sponsored the 2021 law that added G6PD deficiency to the newborn-screening statute, and Gov. Kathy Hochul signed it that December. They followed it with a negotiated chapter amendment defining which babies must be tested. It passed the Senate 63–0 and the Assembly 145–0, Hochul signed it on Feb. 24, 2022, and it took effect June 20, 2022. In 2023 lawmakers passed a bill to test every newborn, but Hochul vetoed it, so the statewide mandate still covers only these at-risk babies.
