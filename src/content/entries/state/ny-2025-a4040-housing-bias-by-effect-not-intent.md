Some housing discrimination doesn't come from a landlord who says no to a group outright. It comes from a rule that sounds neutral but shuts out one group far more than others. Examples include blanket bans on anyone with a criminal record, rigid income minimums that screen out voucher holders, and automated tenant-screening tools. New York's Human Rights Law now says a practice like that can be illegal because of its effect, even if no one meant to discriminate. That includes a practice that keeps neighborhoods segregated.

A landlord or other housing provider can still defend a practice. Once you show a discriminatory effect, the provider has to prove, with real evidence, that the practice is necessary to achieve a substantial, legitimate and nondiscriminatory interest. Even then, you can win by showing that a less discriminatory practice would serve that interest. You don't apply for this protection. It covers housing practices from Dec. 19, 2025 onward, and you can raise it in a complaint or lawsuit.

Federal courts and HUD have long recognized this "disparate impact" standard. But President Trump's first administration moved to weaken HUD's version, and the sponsors wanted the protection in state law in case Washington drops it. Assembly Member Micah Lasher and Sen. Brian Kavanagh carried the bill with Assembly Member Jordan Wright. It passed the Assembly 95–46 and the Senate 39–20 in June 2025, and Gov. Kathy Hochul signed it on Dec. 19, 2025.
