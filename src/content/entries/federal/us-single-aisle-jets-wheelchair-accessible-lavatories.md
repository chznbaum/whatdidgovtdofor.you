If you use a wheelchair, the bathroom on most single-aisle planes is too small to use. Single-aisle planes with at least 125 seats flew 67% of U.S. domestic flights in 2021. This rule changes that for new planes with 125 or more seats. At least one lavatory must be big enough for a wheelchair user and attendant if the plane was ordered after October 3, 2033, delivered after October 2, 2035, or is part of a new type design filed after October 2, 2024.

Some changes came sooner. New single-aisle jets delivered since October 2, 2026 must have grab bars, call buttons and door locks you can reach while seated, and controls you can find by touch and use with one hand. Airlines must also improve the narrow wheelchairs used on board. Airlines don't have to rebuild lavatories on planes already flying, but any lavatory they fully replace must meet the new rules. You don't need to sign up for anything.

The rule grew out of a 2016 advisory committee where airlines, plane makers and disability groups agreed on recommendations. Paralyzed Veterans of America had pushed for accessible airplane bathrooms for more than 30 years. Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg issued the final rule in July 2023 and shortened the timeline from the 18 to 20 years first proposed. In 2026, DOT paused enforcement of one part of the rule, the yearly flight attendant training, while it reviews how often that training must happen. The other requirements still apply.
