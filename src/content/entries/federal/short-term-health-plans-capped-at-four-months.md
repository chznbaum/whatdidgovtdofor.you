Short-term health plans don't have to follow the Affordable Care Act's rules. They can turn down people with pre-existing conditions, and many leave out mental health care, prescription drugs or maternity care. Their deductibles can reach $25,000. A 2018 rule let these plans run for up to three years, so some people bought them thinking they were full coverage. They only found out what was missing after they got sick.

This rule limited new short-term plans sold on or after Sept. 1, 2024 to three months, or four months with a renewal. It also counted another plan sold to the same person by the same insurer or corporate group within 12 months toward that limit. Every plan had to say on its first page and in its ads that it is not comprehensive coverage. Buyers didn't have to do anything; the limits applied to the plans.

President Joe Biden ordered a review of coverage rules in 2021. Health and Human Services Secretary Xavier Becerra, the Labor Department's Lisa Gomez, Treasury's Aviva Aron-Dine and the IRS's Heather Maloy signed the final rule.

On Aug. 7, 2025, the Trump administration said it would not prioritize enforcing the limits while considering a replacement, and HHS encouraged states to do the same. The replacement proposal was still awaiting White House review on Nov. 14, 2026 and had not been published or finalized. Some states set their own limits on these plans.
