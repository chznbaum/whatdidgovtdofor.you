Trichloroethylene, or TCE, is a solvent used in brake cleaners, degreasers, spot removers, sealants, and spray coatings. It is known to cause liver cancer, kidney cancer, and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, and it can harm a developing baby's heart even at very small concentrations. The rule bars retailers from selling TCE or products containing at least 0.1% of it after June 16, 2025. Most industrial and commercial uses were prohibited after September 15, 2025.

You don't need to apply or take action. Consumer products containing regulated amounts of TCE should no longer be on retail shelves. Some industrial uses, including cleaning certain aircraft and medical-device parts and making refrigerants, have longer phaseouts. Many longer-phaseout uses require a written workplace chemical-protection plan and a 0.2 ppm exposure limit. EPA estimates that limit will cut long-term workplace exposure by 97%.

The rule capped a fight that began more than 40 years ago. Anne Anderson, whose son Jimmy developed leukemia amid Woburn's contaminated-water crisis, spent decades pressing for action. Senator Ed Markey championed the TCE ban with her. Michal Freedhoff's EPA chemical safety office developed the rule, and Administrator Michael Regan signed it in December 2024.

Industry challenges briefly paused the rule in early 2025; the Third Circuit let most of it take effect on March 28. EPA later postponed the conditions attached to narrow critical-use exemptions until that litigation ends. The agency is also reconsidering parts of the rule, and in October 2026 it proposed delaying several remaining deadlines through the end of 2027.
