Congress wrote permanent yearly funding into law for historically Black colleges, tribal colleges and five other types of minority-serving colleges. It split $255 million a year this way: $85 million for HBCUs, $100 million for Hispanic-serving colleges, $30 million for tribal colleges and $40 million for other minority-serving colleges. The money goes to the schools, so students don't apply. HBCUs are under 3% of U.S. colleges, but they produce almost 20% of Black graduates.

The old funding ran out on Sept. 30, 2019, and some schools started warning staff about cuts. Rep. Alma Adams and Rep. Mark Walker introduced a two-year extension in May. Sen. Doug Jones led it in the Senate. Sen. Lamar Alexander, a Republican, and Sen. Patty Murray, a Democrat, then made a deal to make the money permanent. Sen. Tim Scott led the Senate amendment, which Sens. Richard Burr and Chris Coons also sponsored. The deal paid for the money by simplifying the FAFSA. The Education Department can now get applicants' tax information straight from the IRS instead of asking for it again. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that change saves $2.8 billion over ten years.

The House passed it 319–96, and the Senate passed it without objection hours later. President Donald Trump signed it on December 19, 2019. In September 2026, the Education Department redirected the FY2025 and FY2026 shares meant for Hispanic-serving and several other minority-serving colleges to HBCUs and tribal colleges.
