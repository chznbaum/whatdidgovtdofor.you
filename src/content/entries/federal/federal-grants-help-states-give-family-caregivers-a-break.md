Caring for a child or adult relative with a disability or serious illness can be a round-the-clock job. Respite care gives the caregiver a short break while someone else steps in. This law renewed the federal Lifespan Respite Care Program, which gives competitive grants to states to build and expand respite services. It authorized $10 million a year for 2020 through 2024. It also required states that get grants to report data so the Department of Health and Human Services can see what works.

You don't apply to the federal program yourself; your state does. States use the money in different ways. Some offer vouchers so families can pick their own respite provider. Others train and recruit respite workers or keep directories that help families find care. Agencies in 39 states and D.C. have received grants since 2009.

Rep. Jim Langevin of Rhode Island, who co-wrote the original 2006 law, introduced the bill with Rep. Cathy McMorris Rodgers of Washington. Sens. Susan Collins and Tammy Baldwin led it in the Senate, so it had sponsors from both parties in each chamber. It passed the House and Senate unanimously, and President Donald Trump signed it on January 5, 2021. In February 2026, Congress renewed the program again through 2030 and made clear that caregivers under 18 count as family caregivers too.
