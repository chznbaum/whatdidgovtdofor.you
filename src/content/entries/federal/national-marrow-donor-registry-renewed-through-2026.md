Some people with leukemia, sickle cell disease or another serious blood disorder need a blood stem cell transplant, and often no one in their family is a match. Then they depend on a national program that matches them with an unrelated volunteer donor or with donated umbilical cord blood. This law renewed that federal work, known as the C.W. Bill Young Cell Transplantation Program. The nonprofit NMDP operates its donor registry under federal contracts. The law also renewed the National Cord Blood Inventory, whose participating public banks collect and store donated cord blood.

The law authorized $31 million a year for the transplant program and $23 million a year for cord blood, for fiscal years 2022 through 2026. It also requires a review at least every two years of new treatments that use stem cells. Patients do not apply to the registry themselves: their doctor or transplant center searches it for them. In 2021, more than 22 million Americans were registered as potential donors, according to Sen. Jack Reed's office.

Rep. Doris Matsui (D) introduced the House bill with Reps. Gus Bilirakis (R) and Chellie Pingree (D), and it passed 415–2. Reed led the Senate version with Sens. Richard Burr (R), Tina Smith (D) and Tim Scott (R); Sen. Tammy Duckworth (D) later joined as a cosponsor. The Senate passed the House bill by voice vote, and President Joe Biden signed it on May 26, 2021.

The law's five-year authorization of appropriations ended on Sept. 30, 2026, but the matching program is still operating. A stopgap spending law continues fiscal 2026 projects and activities through Dec. 11. A new five-year renewal passed the House in July 2026 and is waiting for a Senate vote.
