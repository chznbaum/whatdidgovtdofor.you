If you're a qualified worker with a limitation related to pregnancy, childbirth, or a related medical condition, your employer must make reasonable changes after you tell it what you need. Examples include breaks, a stool, lighter duty, telework, schedule changes, or time off for appointments and recovery. It can refuse if the change would cause undue hardship, but it can't force you onto leave when another reasonable change would let you work.

The law generally covers private, state and local government employers with 15 or more workers, plus federal agencies, Congress, employment agencies and unions. You don't apply to a government program. If your employer refuses, you can generally file an EEOC charge.

Sen. Bob Casey introduced the Senate bill in 2012 and reintroduced it four times. Rep. Jerrold Nadler introduced the House bill in 2012 and sponsored it in every Congress through 2021, when the House passed it 315–101. In December 2022, Republican Sen. Bill Cassidy offered it with Casey as an amendment to the year-end spending bill. The Senate added it 73–24, and President Joe Biden signed the bill December 29, 2022.

The law still applies for most workers, but its reach is contested. In May 2025, a judge vacated the rule for purely elective abortions not needed to treat a pregnancy-related condition. Federal enforcement is blocked against Texas state agencies while the full Fifth Circuit reconsiders Texas's challenge. The EEOC has also scheduled a proposal to narrow which related medical conditions its rule covers.
