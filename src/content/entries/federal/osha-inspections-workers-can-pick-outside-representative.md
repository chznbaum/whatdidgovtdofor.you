When an OSHA safety inspector walks through your workplace, workers have the right to send a representative along. This rule makes clear that the representative doesn't have to work for your employer. It can be a union rep, a safety expert, or someone who speaks your language. The inspector must decide there's good cause for the person to be there: for example, that they know the hazards in your kind of work or can help workers who don't speak English talk to the inspector.

You don't need a union to use it. OSHA said workers without a recognized union can also choose a representative. The inspector can still turn away someone who disrupts the inspection, and the limits that protect trade secrets still apply. Their job is to accompany the inspector, not to run the inspection.

OSHA said it made the change because workers often tell inspectors more when someone they trust is beside them, and outside experts can spot hazards inspectors might miss. In one example the agency cited, a representative who could help with language let OSHA interview many workers who weren't fluent in English. Doug Parker, OSHA's head under President Joe Biden, issued the rule after reviewing more than 11,000 public comments. It took effect May 31, 2024. Business groups led by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce sued to overturn it, and as of early 2026 that case was still pending.
