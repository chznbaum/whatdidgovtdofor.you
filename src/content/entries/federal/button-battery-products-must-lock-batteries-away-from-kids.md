Button and coin batteries power remotes, key fobs, holiday lights and other household products. If swallowed, one can burn through a child's throat within hours. Reese's Law required safety rules for covered products that use them. Replaceable-battery compartments must need a tool or two separate movements at once to open. Compartments also must keep batteries from coming loose in drop tests. Product packaging and instructions must carry ingestion warnings; the product itself must when practicable. Loose batteries need child-resistant packaging and warnings. Toys for children under 14 that meet the federal toy standard are exempt from this product rule. You do not have to apply: manufacturers and importers must comply, and regulators recall products that break the rules.

The law is named for Reese Hamsmith, an 18-month-old from Texas who died in December 2020 after swallowing a battery from a remote. Her mother, Trista Hamsmith, founded Reese's Purpose and took the fight to Congress. Rep. Robin Kelly (D) wrote the House bill with Reps. Jodey Arrington (R) and Ted Lieu (D). Sens. Richard Blumenthal (D) and Marsha Blackburn (R) carried it in the Senate. It passed the House on July 27, 2022, and the Senate passed it without objection on August 2. President Joe Biden signed it on August 16. The Consumer Product Safety Commission, chaired by Alexander Hoehn-Saric, voted 4-0 on September 8, 2023 to adopt the rules. The product rule took effect October 23, and CPSC's enforcement grace period ended March 19, 2024.
