When a child can't stay safely at home, a grandparent, aunt or close family friend often takes them in. Before this rule, many of these caregivers never got a foster license, either because the standards were written for strangers or because the process took months. Without a license, they got little or no foster care payment. In comments on the rule, one commenter described an unlicensed relative caring for a child on $388 a month.

The rule changes this in two ways. First, it lets each state, territory or tribe write separate licensing standards tailored to relatives and other kin. The standards can remove non-safety barriers, but homes still must meet the federal requirements that apply to all foster homes, including background checks and safety standards. Federal foster care money can pay for otherwise eligible children in homes licensed under those standards. Second, the rule requires licensed or approved kin homes to receive the same foster care maintenance payment a non-kin foster home would receive for that child. Caregivers must go through their child welfare agency's licensing or approval process; the rule does not make unlicensed homes eligible. Adopting separate standards is optional, and more than 20 states and tribes have done so. Iowa started in July 2025, and its kin license takes about two months, compared with six to nine months for a standard one.

The Administration for Children and Families, led by Acting Assistant Secretary Jeff Hild, issued the rule; Hild approved the document, and HHS Secretary Xavier Becerra signed it in September 2023. ACF estimated that the rule would cost the federal government $3.085 billion in foster care payments and administration over 10 years.
