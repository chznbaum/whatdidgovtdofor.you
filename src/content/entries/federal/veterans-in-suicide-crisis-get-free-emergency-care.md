If you're a veteran in an acute suicidal crisis, go to the nearest ER, VA or not, and VA pays. That includes the ER visit, up to 30 days in a hospital or crisis residential program, or up to 90 days of outpatient care when appropriate, plus emergency transportation. VA can extend care if you're still in crisis. You pay no copay.

You don't have to be enrolled in VA health care. Veterans who meet the service and discharge rules qualify, along with some former service members with other-than-honorable discharges. That includes people who served 100 cumulative days in a combat zone or in support of a contingency operation, or experienced military sexual trauma. Tell the hospital you're a veteran, and notify VA within seven days. VA then checks other benefit eligibility and refers you for follow-up care. VA data show 15,218 people used it in fiscal 2025.

Rep. Mark Takano, then House Veterans' Affairs chair, proposed free crisis care in his Veterans ACCESS Act in 2020. It became part of his COMPACT Act after the House and Senate committee leaders—Takano, Jerry Moran, Jon Tester and Phil Roe—reached a deal to pass it alongside a Senate suicide-prevention bill. Roe co-managed the House bill. The House passed it by voice vote that September; the Senate followed by unanimous consent in November. President Donald Trump signed it Dec. 5, 2020. The law gave VA 270 days to start, but VA under Secretary Denis McDonough launched the benefit Jan. 17, 2023.
