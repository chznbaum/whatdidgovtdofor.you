Adult Protective Services, or APS, steps in when an older adult or an adult with a disability is being abused, neglected, sexually abused, financially exploited, or is unable to meet essential self-care needs. Each state runs its own APS program, and until 2024 there were no federal rules setting minimum standards. This rule sets standards nationwide.

After APS screens a report in, an immediate-risk case—one that is life-threatening or likely to cause irreparable harm or significant loss of income, assets, or resources—must receive an initial response within 24 hours. APS can arrange that response through emergency services, police, or another round-the-clock partner. Every state must keep at least two reporting methods available 24/7, including one online. That does not mean live staff must answer at all hours; off-hours messages may be retrieved the next business day. APS must also use person-directed planning, consider the least restrictive options, guard against conflicts of interest, and coordinate with Medicaid, long-term care ombudsmen, and law enforcement.

You do not have to apply for anything. If you or someone you know needs help, contact your state’s APS program. States receiving federal Elder Justice Act grants must fully meet the standards by May 8, 2028.

The Administration for Community Living, then led by Alison Barkoff, developed and issued the rule. HHS Secretary Xavier Becerra signed it. Congress also funded state formula grants to help states strengthen their APS programs.
