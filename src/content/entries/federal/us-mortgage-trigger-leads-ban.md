When you apply for a mortgage, the lender pulls your credit report. Credit bureaus used to sell news of that check as a "trigger lead" to other lenders and brokers, who then rushed to call, text and email you. A mortgage brokers' group said some applicants got more than 100 of these messages within a day, many of them misleading. This law stops most of those sales.

A credit bureau can now pass your information along only if you authorized it, or if the recipient originated or services your current mortgage, or is an insured bank or credit union where you have an account. Any permitted trigger lead must also support a firm offer of credit or insurance. You don't need to do anything to be protected. The rules took effect March 4, 2026, 180 days after the law was signed. The law also had Congress's watchdog, the Government Accountability Office, study whether trigger leads sent by text message do consumers any good.

Rep. John Rose, a Tennessee Republican, and Rep. Ritchie Torres, a New York Democrat, carried this version over two Congresses. Sens. Jack Reed, a Rhode Island Democrat, and Bill Hagerty, a Tennessee Republican, introduced the companion Senate bill. Former Rep. Lacy Clay, a Missouri Democrat, had paved the way with an opt-in bill in 2020. The House Financial Services Committee advanced H.R. 2808 by 46–0, the House passed it by voice vote in June 2025, and the Senate passed it by unanimous consent that August. Banking and consumer groups backed it. President Donald Trump signed it on September 5, 2025.
