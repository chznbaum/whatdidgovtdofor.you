Diesel exhaust from big trucks and buses releases nitrogen oxides and soot, which form smog and make asthma, heart disease, and other illnesses worse. This rule makes the limits on that pollution from new heavy-duty engines more than 80% stronger, starting with model year 2027. It also requires engines to meet the limits for 1.5 to 2.5 times as many years on the road and stretches emissions warranties 2.8 to 4.5 times longer, so trucks don't get dirtier as they age.

You don't have to do anything to benefit. Cleaner trucks replace older ones over time, so the gains grow each year. By 2045, EPA estimates up to 2,900 fewer early deaths, 18,000 fewer cases of childhood asthma, 1.1 million fewer missed school days, and 78,000 fewer missed work days each year. The biggest gains go to the roughly 72 million people living near truck freight routes. EPA puts the yearly benefits at up to $29 billion more than the costs.

EPA Administrator Michael Regan finalized the standards in December 2022. They were the first tightening of these truck limits in more than two decades. In July 2026, EPA under Administrator Lee Zeldin proposed keeping the pollution limits but pushing back the longer clean-engine lifetimes to 2030, dropping the longer warranties, and letting manufacturers sell certain medium and heavy-duty engines that miss the limits but remain below an upper limit if they pay penalties. That change hadn't been finalized as of November 2026.
