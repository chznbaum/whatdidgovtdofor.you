Traffickers often force the people they control to commit crimes. A criminal record can block work, housing, or school long after a survivor gets free. This law lets survivors ask a federal court to vacate a nonviolent conviction that resulted directly from being trafficked. They may also seek expungement of trafficking-related federal arrests. Violent-offense arrests qualify only after acquittal, dismissal, no prosecution, or reduction to an eligible nonviolent charge. A vacated conviction becomes an acquittal, and related official records are expunged.

The survivor or a lawyer must file a written motion. Filing is free, and the motion and related papers stay under seal. A statement from an anti-trafficking provider or clinician can be enough if it is credible and no other evidence is readily available. Judges can reduce a prison term when the offense resulted directly from trafficking. Defendants can also raise a trafficking-based duress defense. Justice Department legal-representation grants may cover post-conviction relief. The law covers only federal records.

Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand (D) and Rep. Ann Wagner (R) introduced versions in 2016 and kept pushing them. Rep. Russell Fry (R) sponsored the House bill that became law, co-led by Wagner and Rep. Hank Johnson (D). Rep. Ted Lieu (D) also helped secure passage, while Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith (R) co-led the Senate companion. The House passed it by voice vote and the Senate by unanimous consent. President Donald Trump signed it on January 23, 2026.
