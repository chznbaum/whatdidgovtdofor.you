Abusers can use a shared family phone plan to track, watch, or control the people on it. If you're an adult survivor—or an adult caring for someone who experienced abuse—your phone company generally must separate your line, and lines used by people in your care, from a plan shared with the abuser. It can't charge a fee or penalty. Within two business days after a completed request, the company must separate the lines or explain why an operational or technical problem prevents it and give you alternatives. You'll need evidence such as a police report, protective order, or signed affidavit from a licensed health care provider, social worker, victim services provider, or court employee. Separating a line used by someone in your care also requires a signed affidavit identifying that person and line. After the split, you pay for your own line going forward.

If money is tight, proof of your request also qualifies you for up to six months of emergency Lifeline help: up to $9.25 a month off phone or internet service, and more on Tribal lands. You'll need to be in a program like SNAP or Medicaid, or have an income at or below 200% of the federal poverty line. You apply at LifelineSupport.org. Phone companies must also keep calls and texts to domestic violence hotlines off the call logs your abuser can see.

Rep. Annie Kuster wrote the bill with Rep. Anna Eshoo, and Republican Rep. Tim Walberg joined them. Sens. Brian Schatz and Deb Fischer led the Senate version. The bill passed the House with support from both parties and passed the Senate with no objection. President Joe Biden signed it on December 7, 2022. The FCC, led by Jessica Rosenworcel, adopted the rules in November 2023. Phone companies had to start separating lines on July 15, 2024.
