Since 1963, the Supreme Court has required prosecutors to turn over evidence that favors the person they've charged and is material to the case, under a case called Brady v. Maryland. Prosecutors don't always do it. The 2008 corruption conviction of Alaska Sen. Ted Stevens was thrown out after it came to light that prosecutors and FBI agents had hidden helpful evidence. According to the National Registry of Exonerations, prosecutors hid that kind of evidence at trial in half of all murder exonerations from 1989 to 2017.

This law changed the federal court rules. At the first scheduled court date in every federal criminal case where both the prosecutor and defense counsel are present, the judge must issue an oral and written order confirming the prosecutor's duty to share that evidence. The order also lays out what can happen if prosecutors don't comply. Each federal circuit's judicial council writes a model order that judges can use. You don't have to ask for the order; once both lawyers are present, the judge has to issue it.

Sen. Dan Sullivan, an Alaska Republican, and Sen. Dick Durbin, an Illinois Democrat, introduced the bill in 2019. Five more senators—three Republicans and two Democrats—joined as cosponsors. The Senate passed it without objection in May 2020, and the House approved it by voice vote that September. President Donald Trump signed it on October 21, 2020.
