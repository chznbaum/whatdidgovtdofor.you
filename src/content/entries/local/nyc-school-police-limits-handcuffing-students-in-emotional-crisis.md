When a child has a mental health crisis at a New York City public school, this law generally puts school staff and the crisis team first. It sets rules for school safety agents and precinct officers. They stay out unless 911, the principal or a crisis team member calls them, or unless they need to step in to stop an imminent and substantial risk of physical injury. When the principal or crisis team calls them, they must ask what staff already tried to calm the child, whether a parent was reached, and whether the child has a known disability.

Unless a student presents an imminent and substantial risk of serious physical injury—a situation the law exempts from these special rules—NYPD personnel may handcuff or otherwise restrain the student only while the student's behavior poses an imminent and substantial risk of physical injury, and only as long as that risk lasts. They may call an ambulance only if someone needs medical attention or the student poses that risk and they determine de-escalation would not work. Their reports must record how they tried to calm the child, whether a parent was told, and whether anyone was hurt. Families don't need to do anything to get these protections.

Before the law, police handled 12,050 of these incidents in four school years, and they sometimes handcuffed children as young as 5. Deputy Speaker Diana Ayala first introduced a predecessor bill in 2020 and reintroduced it in 2022. Council Member Rita Joseph cosponsored it and chaired the Education Committee, which advanced it 13-0. The Council passed it on Dec. 20, 2023. Mayor Eric Adams returned it unsigned, so it became law on Jan. 20, 2024. The NYPD now files a yearly report on training its school safety staff for these calls.
