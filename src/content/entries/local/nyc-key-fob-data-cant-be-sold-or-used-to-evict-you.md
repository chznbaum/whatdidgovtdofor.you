If your NYC apartment building uses a fob, card, app, or biometric scanner, this law limits what the owner and system operator can do. They need your express consent to collect enrollment data and may collect only specified information needed for entry. They generally cannot sell, lease, or disclose it outside limited exceptions. They cannot track users outside the building, use smart-entry information to harass or evict a tenant, or require the system for entry to an apartment.

You don't have to apply. Your landlord must give you a plain-language privacy policy explaining what the system collects, how long it keeps data, and how it protects it. Entry authentication data generally must be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized. Enrollment or reference data generally must be removed, or anonymized if removal would break the system, within 90 days after you permanently move out or withdraw consent. Limited exceptions cover security incidents, debugging, legal obligations, protected speech, and a user's request for longer retention. If your data is sold illegally, a lawful occupant can choose $200 to $1,000 in damages per sale or seek compensatory and possible punitive damages, plus reasonable lawyer's fees and court costs.

Council Member Mark Levine introduced the bill in 2019, and the City Council passed it in April 2021. Mayor Bill de Blasio did not sign or veto it, so it became law on May 30, 2021. Buildings with existing smart-entry systems had until January 1, 2023, to comply.
