If you apply for an affordable apartment in a New York City development that got $1 million or more in city financial help, the developer can't use or consider your credit score. It also can't hold your medical debt, student loan debt, debts in collections, or court judgments over consumer debt against you. The only exception is unpaid collections and judgments that add up to more than $12,000. You don't have to apply for this protection. The developer has to follow it when choosing tenants.

Only one person in your household can have their credit history reviewed: the person the household names as its representative. The developer has to tell that person in writing how their credit will be judged.

Council Member Mark Levine introduced the bill in June 2019. He argued that people shouldn't lose a chance at affordable housing because they've had some tough breaks in the past. That August, the city's housing agency changed its own lottery rules so that applicants could show 12 months of rent payments instead of passing a credit check. The Council passed Levine's bill on Sept. 16, 2020. Mayor Bill de Blasio returned it without signing it, and it became law on Oct. 17, 2020. The law took effect a year later and covers only city financial help given after that date, so some older buildings aren't bound by it.
