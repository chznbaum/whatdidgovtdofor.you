When a New York City child under 18 has an elevated blood lead level, the Health Department has to look for where the lead came from. This law widened that search. Inspectors used to focus on the child's home. Now they must also check any day care, preschool or nursery school where the child spends 10 or more hours a week. They must also check parks and playgrounds where bare soil could be the source; the Parks Department inspects the ones it runs. If they find a lead paint hazard in the child's apartment, they also check other apartments in the building where a baby under one lives.

Families don't have to apply. The investigation starts when a high blood lead test is reported to the city. Inspectors test painted surfaces with an X-ray device and bare soil that could be a source, and help the family order a free city water test kit. When a lead hazard is found in an apartment, the Health Department posts a notice where everyone in the building can see it. Parents also get information about special education services. After the city orders a landlord to remove lead paint, the landlord has 60 days to report test results for every surface in the apartment to the housing department.

Speaker Corey Johnson introduced the bill in 2018 as part of a large package of lead bills. The Council passed it on March 18, 2021. It became law on April 18, 2021, without Mayor Bill de Blasio's signature, and took effect 18 months later.
