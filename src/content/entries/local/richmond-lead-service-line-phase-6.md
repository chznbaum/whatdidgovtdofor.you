Lead can get into drinking water through old pipes, called service lines, that connect a building to the water main. Richmond's inventory lists just over 11,100 of these lines as likely lead and about 28,000 more as unknown. This ordinance puts $7.3 million into the next round of replacements, the program's sixth phase.

The money matters because the city can't use water utility funds to fix pipes on private property, which is the property owner's side of the line. The $7.3 million comes from the federal Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and is administered by the Virginia Department of Health through the Virginia Resources Authority. It is a principal-forgiveness loan the city will not have to repay and is intended for private-property lines. The other $12.7 million in the $20 million Phase 6 award is a low-interest loan the city must repay; it will fund work in the public right-of-way. Before this round, Richmond had replaced nearly 600 lead lines using state grants and federal pandemic relief money.

Richmond is replacing lines block by block, with priority based partly on residents' vulnerability to lead, neighborhood disadvantage and property-owner participation. Owners must authorize work on their side of the line. Federal rules require every lead line under the city's control to be gone by the end of 2037. If you're not sure what your pipe is made of, call the Department of Public Utilities at 804-646-4646.

Mayor Danny Avula's administration brought the funding ordinance to council. Council members Sarah Abubaker and Katherine Jordan co-sponsored it, and the council adopted it 9–0 on September 28, 2026.
