In New York City, an employer, landlord, school or business can't ban or restrict your hair in a way that singles you out because of your race or religion. That covers hair texture, hairstyles such as locs, braids, twists, cornrows, Afros, Bantu knots and fades, hair length, and head coverings such as turbans, hijabs, yarmulkes and head wraps. Making you straighten your hair, cut it or hide it to get a job, keep it, or get a promotion is illegal, and so is harassing you over it.

The rule takes away the usual excuses. A business can't point to customer preference or say your hair looks "unprofessional" or doesn't fit its image. A health or safety reason counts only if it's real. Even then, the business has to consider options in good faith first, like hair nets, ties, or safety gear that fits your hair. If you keep your hair a certain way for your faith, your employer has to work with you on an accommodation unless that would cause real hardship. You don't need to apply for anything. If you're treated unfairly, you can file a complaint with the city's Commission on Human Rights.

The Commission, chaired by Carmelyn Malalis, first spelled out these protections in 2019 enforcement guidance on hair discrimination against Black New Yorkers. It then wrote them into a formal rule that also covers religion. The Commission voted unanimously to adopt the rule on Dec. 14, 2020, and it took effect Jan. 30, 2021.
