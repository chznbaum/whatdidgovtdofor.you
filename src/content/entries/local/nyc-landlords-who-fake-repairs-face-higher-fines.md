When a city inspector cites your building for a problem like no heat, mold, or a broken lock, the owner can tell the city it has been fixed. Too often it hasn't. This law raised penalties for leaving many housing code violations uncorrected and added extra scrutiny for buildings with repeated false certifications. For violations issued since December 8, 2023, fines are often several times higher than before. In a building with more than five units, many immediately hazardous violations can now cost the owner up to $1,200 a day, up from $125.

The law also created a Certification Watchlist. Every January since 2025, HPD has picked 100 buildings where owners certified more than 20 non-pest hazardous or immediately hazardous violations as corrected and inspectors found at least four of those certifications false. For that calendar year, HPD cannot close a non-pest hazardous or immediately hazardous violation based only on the owner's certification until it has tried at least twice to re-inspect. HPD posts a notice in each listed building so tenants know. Tenants don't have to apply for anything.

Public Advocate Jumaane Williams sponsored the bill, which grew out of his office's yearly Worst Landlord Watchlist. Council Member Pierina Sanchez, who chaired the housing committee, co-sponsored it, and the Council passed it in May 2023. Mayor Eric Adams didn't sign it, and it became law without his signature in June 2023.
